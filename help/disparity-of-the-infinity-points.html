Disparity of the Infinity Points

How far apart a pair's farthest points stand when its nearest point is set at the window, as a share of the picture's width: the whole difference, from near to far, between the two eyes. It measures how much depth a pair holds, and how much it asks of the eyes that view it.

The rule of one-thirtieth

It is customary that the figure should not exceed one-thirtieth of the picture's width — 3.3% — for comfortable viewing; for very large screens, one-fortieth. It is an agreement among stereographers rather than a constant of nature, and a pair a little over it may still be viewed with ease.

How it is measured

Stereo Align reports it beside its correction, and Continuous Shoot's Find Pair aims for it; both measure it alike, from the sideways shift of every detail between the eyes once vertical errors are removed. It does not depend on where the window is afterwards set.

So that a few false matches, or a leaf stirred by the wind, should not decide it, the extreme 2% at either end is set aside. Where the nearest or farthest object is very small — a distant skyline, a single near branch — the figure therefore reads somewhat low, and it is advisable to allow a margin under the rule. StereoPhoto Maker's figure may likewise read low, being drawn from the points it chose for alignment.

History

The measure is older than its present name. Stereographers have long called it the deviation: the difference between the separation of the near point and that of the far point.

  • In 1930 Hermann Lüscher, writing in Der Stereoskopiker, set the limit as an angle: the difference in viewing angle between the nearest and the farthest point should not exceed 60 to 70 minutes of arc — the "Lüscher angle".
  • The International Stereoscopic Union states the limit in the picture itself: the deviation should not exceed 1/30 of the entire image width, a value that follows from the 70′ condition, and 1/40 on very large cinema screens.
  • On miniature film the rule is given as 1.2 mm, one-thirtieth of the 36 mm frame. The depth-of-field scale of the Stereo Realist, whose format became the standard for stereo slides, has been read as a deviation scale: its f/8 marks correspond to 1.2 mm, and the more cautious f/5.6 marks, preferred by Charles Piper for projection, to 0.8 mm.

The name "disparity of the infinity points" is StereoPhoto Maker's, which reports the figure after its Auto Alignment.
